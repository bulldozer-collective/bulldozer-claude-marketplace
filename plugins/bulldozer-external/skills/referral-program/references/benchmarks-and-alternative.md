# Referral benchmarks — the citation trail, and why to run your own cohort

The often-cited **16–25% LTV lift** for referred customers traces back to one real academic study: Schmitt, Skiera & Van den Bulte, "Referral Programs and Customer Value," *Journal of Marketing*, 2011 — a German bank, ~10,000 customers, ~3-year follow-up. Treat that range as directional, not as a number to promise a client — it comes from one company's referral program, one product category, one country.

The churn and re-referral multipliers that circulate alongside it (often quoted as **"18–37% lower churn"** and **"refer 2–3x more"**) don't trace back to any single audited source once you follow the citation chain — several vendor blogs repeat them attached to the same 2011 paper without the paper actually saying that.

A later replication (Armelini, Barrot & Becker, *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, 2015) found referred customers do churn less, but the effect looks driven by demographic similarity to the referrer rather than by the referral channel itself — a reason to distrust a flat multiplier even for the LTV figure.

**Run your own referred-vs-non-referred cohort** (activation, retention, revenue) before quoting any of these numbers internally — not the 2011 bank's numbers, not a vendor blog's restatement of them.

## The alternative you're really comparing to

It's rarely a competitor's referral program — it's the unincentivized word-of-mouth already happening for free. Before building the mechanism, get a baseline: ask new customers how they heard about you, or check the free-text `source` field on the lead lists this skill sets up (see `references/measuring-in-bulldozer-os.md` — already used for other attribution). Without that baseline, a program that looks like it's "working" may just be paying for referrals that would have happened anyway — money spent buying credit for word-of-mouth you already had, not lifting the volume.
