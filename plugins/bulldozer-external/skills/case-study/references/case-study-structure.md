# Case Study Structure — Sections and Format Variants

## Contents

- 1. Headline + Snapshot Box
- 2. The Problem (300-400 words)
- 3. How They Did It (300-400 words)
- 4. The Results (400-500 words)
- 5. Pull Quotes
- 6. Format Variants

### 1. Headline + Snapshot Box

The headline leads with the result: *"How [Customer] Cut Invoice Processing Time by 94%"* — not *"[Customer] Partners with [Company] to Transform Operations."*

The snapshot box appears immediately below the headline, before the narrative begins. It travels when the case study gets forwarded in Slack.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  [Customer Company]                              │
│  Industry: [X] · Size: [Y employees]            │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  94%  reduction in processing time              │
│  $127K  saved in first 12 months                │
│  2 days  to full implementation                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Three metrics maximum. The first is the headline metric (most impressive). The second is financial (gives CFOs a number). The third is ease-of-adoption (handles the "is this realistic for us?" objection).

### 2. The Problem (300-400 words)

Describe the before state in the customer's words, not yours. The goal: the ideal prospect reads this section and thinks *"that's exactly what we're dealing with."*

Structure:
- Company context (2 sentences — industry, size, what they do)
- The specific situation before: what was broken, how broken, in what numbers
- What they'd tried: previous attempts, why they failed
- The tipping point: what made them start looking for a solution at that particular moment
- At least one direct quote from the customer about how the problem felt

**Anti-pattern to avoid:** Describing the problem in vendor language. "They lacked visibility into their operational metrics" is vendor language. "Every Monday morning, the ops team spent 4 hours pulling numbers from three different spreadsheets just to answer a question the CEO asked the week before" is customer language.

---

### 3. How They Did It (300-400 words)

Not a product brochure. The customer is the protagonist — your product is the vehicle.

Structure:
- Why they chose this solution (what tipped the decision, what the evaluation process was)
- How they got started (implementation timeline, who was involved, what was harder or easier than expected)
- How they use it now (specific workflows, specific features used by name — not "they used the platform" but "they used the Rules Engine to automate X")
- Adoption experience (did the team embrace it? was there resistance? how was it handled?)

**Objection handling opportunity:** Implementation, change management, and user adoption are the three fears every buyer carries. Address all three here, directly and honestly. "Implementation took 2 weeks, not the 1 week we hoped for, because we had 5 years of legacy data to migrate" is more credible than "seamless implementation."

---

### 4. The Results (400-500 words)

Lead with the most impressive metric. Then layer.

Structure:
- Headline metric (with timeframe)
- Secondary metrics (financial, team, customer impact)
- Quote from the economic buyer (not just the champion)
- The compound effect: what is the team doing with the recovered time/money?
- What's next: how they plan to expand or build on what they've accomplished

**The "what's next" close:** Ending a case study with a forward-looking statement signals momentum — this isn't a one-time fix but a compounding investment. It also handles the "will this still work 12 months from now?" objection implicitly.

---

### 5. Pull Quotes

Extract 2–3 strong quotes from the transcript and format them as pull quotes — set apart from
the body text, attributed by name and title, placed between sections to break up long blocks
of dense reading.

**What makes a quote worth pulling:**
- It says something the writer couldn't say credibly in their own voice ("I was skeptical
  this would actually save us time" lands from the customer; it reads as spin from you).
- It names a feeling, not just a fact. "We stopped dreading Monday mornings" does work that
  "the process became more efficient" cannot.
- It comes from the highest-authority speaker available for that specific claim. A claim
  about ROI is more credible from the economic buyer than from the champion, even if the
  champion said it first.

**Priority order when time with the transcript is limited:**
1. One quote from the economic buyer, if the interview included them — this is the line sales
   reps lean on to answer "will my boss find this credible?"
2. One quote from the champion about the before state, in their own words — this is what a
   prospect in the same situation recognizes as their own problem.
3. One quote about the unexpected or the emotional shift — surprise reads as authenticity.

**Format standard:**

```
"[Verbatim quote, cleaned up only for filler words — never rewritten into cleaner corporate
phrasing. That rewrite is exactly what erases the trust signal a customer's own words carry.]"
— [Name], [Title], [Company]
```

If a quote needs shortening, cut with an ellipsis and keep the remaining words exactly as
spoken. Paraphrasing a quote to make it "read better" produces a sentence that sounds like
every other case study on the internet — the opposite of what a quote is there to do.

---

### 6. Format Variants

The interview produces one transcript; the case study produces several assets from it. Do not
draft these separately — pull them from the approved long-form version so every format says
the same thing in a different length, and nothing new needs its own approval round.

**Sales deck excerpt (≤200 words):** headline metric, the snapshot box, one pull quote —
prioritize the economic buyer's if there is one — and a single line pointing to the full case
study. This is what a rep pastes directly into a deck slide or a follow-up email; it has to
stand on its own without the reader clicking through to anything.

**LinkedIn post variant:** built from the case study's material, not a link to it. A post that
sends readers off-platform gets shown to fewer people than one that delivers value inside the
post itself — that incentive is structural and hasn't gone away. Lead with the headline result
in the first line, before the "see more" cut, then the before/after in three or four short
lines, then one pull quote. Don't route around this by dropping the link in the first comment
instead of the body — that workaround stopped working once the platform started treating a
link in the first comment as the same bridge-behavior signal as a link in the post, penalizing
reach either way. Make the post stand on its own with no link at all, and if a click-through
matters, point people to a comment or a profile mention rather than a bare URL. Don't quote a
precise reach-loss number for this to a client: independent measurements of the same 2026
period disagree wildly on the size of the hit — one large study puts it under 20%, another
around 25%, several put it near 60%, and at least one finds posts with a couple of links
outperforming — while agreeing on the direction for a post whose only purpose is to route
traffic out. The rule is the format decision (deliver the value in the post), not a number to
defend in a client meeting.

**Publishing through Bulldozer OS:** the LinkedIn tool posts to the feed of whichever person's
LinkedIn account is connected to the project — there is no company-page path through the OS, so
decide upfront whose profile carries this (often the account's own rep, if a personal
relationship reads as more credible than a brand post) rather than assuming it lands on the
company page. That constraint may work in this skill's favor on the reach-penalty question
above: one of the independent measurements already cited (Ordinal, 900,000+ posts) reports the
external-link penalty concentrated on company pages, with personal profiles showing almost no
measurable hit. LinkedIn has never confirmed a downranking mechanism either way, and this is one
source among several that disagree on magnitude — not a reason to quote a number to the client —
but it's a real point in favor of a personal profile being the lower-risk choice when a project
has one credible option for whose feed carries the post. If the case study is going into the CMS as a collection item rather than a
hand-built page — most sites keep case studies in a blog-like collection, not a static page —
the OS can write it end-to-end: pull the collection's field schema first (fieldData is keyed by
field slug, not by label), and know the item is created as a draft by default, so publishing it
live is a separate, deliberate step, not something that happens the moment it's written.
