# Buyer Tactics — Names, Signs and Counters

Procurement and legal run the same handful of hardball moves on most deals at this stage.
None of them require a clever counter invented on the spot — naming the tactic to yourself
(not necessarily out loud) is usually enough to stop reacting to it as if it were new information.

| Tactic | What it looks like | The counter |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher authority** | "I need to check with my boss/finance" after you've already given ground — used to extract one more concession without giving anything back | Get the actual decision-maker on the call (see above) so there's no invisible third party left to appeal to |
| **Nibbling** | A small, unrelated ask tacked on right before signature — an extra seat, a training day, a faster SLA — framed as too minor to hold up the deal | Write down every open item as it comes up; before the call ends, ask "is this the complete list, or is there anything else that needs to be settled?" A nibble that's named stops being free |
| **Good cop / bad cop** | One buyer-side contact is warm and reasonable, another (often legal or a senior exec) is the one blocking — makes the "reasonable" number look better by contrast | Don't renegotiate with the good cop what was already agreed with the bad cop — refer back to the last confirmed position instead of treating it as a fresh ask |
| **Deadline pressure** | "We need this signed by Friday or the budget disappears" — sometimes real, often manufactured to rush you past your floor | Ask what happens on Saturday if it isn't signed. A deadline nobody can explain the consequence of is pressure, not a fact |
| **Exploding / best-and-final** | "This is my final offer, take it or the deal's off" from the buyer side — designed to stop you from countering | Take it at face value only if it's consistent with everything else they've said. If it isn't, ask a genuine question about what changed rather than either folding or calling the bluff |

Vendors run the mirror image of some of these too — don't do it yourself. Never manufacture
a deadline you don't intend to hold ("this pricing expires tomorrow" when it won't), and
never use a fake "let me check with my manager" to stall for time — it's the same tactic
a buyer would run on you, and it costs the same trust when it's discovered.
