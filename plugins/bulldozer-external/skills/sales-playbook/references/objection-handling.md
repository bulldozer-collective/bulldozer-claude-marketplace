# Objection Handling

## Step 5: Objection Handling

Use a four-step read: **Acknowledge → Clarify → Respond → Confirm**. It's the same logic taught under the LAER / LAARC lineage (Carew International's Listen-Acknowledge-Explore-Respond, extended with a Confirm step) — the label here is shortened to what a rep needs mid-call, not a claim to a separate branded method.

1. **Acknowledge** — Show you heard them. "I understand why you'd feel that way."
2. **Clarify** — Probe the real concern. "Help me understand — what specifically concerns you about...?"
3. **Respond** — Address the actual concern with evidence (not defensiveness)
4. **Confirm** — Check it landed. "Does that address your concern, or is there something else underneath it?"

**Top objections and scripts:**

Treat the scripts below as the starting grid, not the ceiling. If call recordings are connected (Claap or equivalent), the objections reps actually hit are pulled from real transcripts rather than guessed — recordings are auto-tagged across four categories (`Objections`, `PainPoints`, `CompetitorMentions`, `FeatureRequests`), each filterable by deal, and each useful for a different part of this playbook, not only the objections list below. Once there's enough call volume to see a repeated pattern (not from a single call), fold what's actually said into the list below rather than leaving it as an assumption.

**"It's too expensive."**  
Clarify: "Is this about the total number, the per-seat cost, or how it fits your current budget cycle?"  
Respond: "Our average customer sees [specific ROI outcome] within [timeframe]. At your current [cost of the problem], the payback period is [X months]. Would it help if I put together the business case in writing?"

**"We're not ready to move forward right now."**  
Clarify: "Is that a timing issue, a priority issue, or is there a concern about the fit I haven't addressed?"  
Respond: "What would need to be true for this to become a priority?" If the answer names a real event (renewal date, budget cycle, new hire starting), log it and set a dated follow-up. If the answer is vague, treat it as a soft no — qualify the deal down rather than leaving it open indefinitely.  
Confirm: "So if [the thing they named] happens, does it make sense to reconnect then, or is there something else holding this back?"

**"I need to check with my team / my boss."**  
Clarify: "Who else needs to weigh in, and what will they be looking for?"  
Respond: "That makes sense — what's usually the sticking point when [that stakeholder] reviews something like this?" Offer to join that conversation directly: "Would it help if I sent a short summary they could read in two minutes, or joined the call myself?"  
Confirm: A rep who accepts "I'll check and get back to you" without a stakeholder name and a date has lost control of the deal. Never end this objection without both.  
Read the timing, not just the words: early in discovery, this sentence is usually a real approval step to map — more MEDDPICC to gather, not a stall. Late in negotiation, right after a round of concessions, the same sentence can be "appeal to higher authority" — a documented hardball move (see Step 6) used to bank what's already been given without anyone committing back. Same script, different read depending on where in the cycle it lands.

**"We're happy with what we have."**  
Clarify: "What's working well with your current setup? And is there anything that's a stretch or that you've had to work around?"  
Respond: Don't attack the incumbent. Anchor on the specific pain surfaced in discovery: "You mentioned [pain] was costing you [impact] — does your current solution address that today, or is that still open?" If recordings are connected (Claap or equivalent), pull transcripts tagged `CompetitorMentions` for this account first — ground the response in what the prospect actually said about the incumbent, not a guessed comparison.  
Confirm: If the prospect can't name a gap, they are not a real opportunity yet — qualify out rather than chase a status-quo deal.

**Silence after a proposal is not agreement.** A prospect who goes quiet after a proposal has not tacitly accepted it — reps who wait it out lose deals that were still winnable. Closing a deal typically takes more than one round of objections after the proposal goes out, not a single email; a rep who stops following up after the first unanswered message lets the deal die by default rather than by decision. Re-engage with a specific, open verification question rather than a check-in ping — "Is the [business case / timeline / stakeholder buy-in] still holding this up, or has something changed?" — and if two rounds of that produce nothing, offer a clean exit: "Should I close this out for now and reconnect when the timing is better?" A clear no is more useful than a deal rotting in the pipeline.

**Logging why a deal is lost.** HubSpot's native "Closed Lost Reason" field is free text, not a fixed list — leave it as the only place reps record a loss and six months from now nobody can tell whether "price" or "timing" or "went with a competitor" is actually the biggest reason deals die, because every rep phrases it differently. Don't just flip the native field's type to a dropdown either: HubSpot blocks re-saving a deal that still carries the old free-text value once the field is converted, so every deal closed before the conversion looks stuck with a required-but-unfillable field. Use (or ask RevOps to set up) a separate curated picklist property instead — a closed, short set a rep picks from, not a sentence they type, and not a conversion of the field reps have already been typing into. If "Competitor" is one of the options in that picklist, pair it with a second field naming which competitor, from the same closed list tracked for battlecards — otherwise "lost to a competitor" is recorded but a quarterly pull of every deal lost to one named competitor is still a manual read of free-text notes, not a query. A rep who marks a deal Closed Lost without picking one of those reasons hasn't actually closed the loop.
