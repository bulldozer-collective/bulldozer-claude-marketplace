# Pipeline Health Audit — Stages, Closed Lost Reasons, Forecasts

## Pipeline Health Audit

Every check below is scoped to **one pipeline at a time.** A HubSpot portal that has been in production for more than a year is rarely on a single deal pipeline — sales, upsell, outbound prospecting, partner or booking pipelines commonly run side by side, each with its own stage set. `dealstage` is one shared enumeration underneath all of them, so the raw picklist can show a sales pipeline's "Verbal Commitment" next to an outbound pipeline's "NRP 2" and a booking pipeline's "Tourné" — stages that measure entirely different things. Grouping deals by stage, or computing a closing rate, without filtering to a single `pipeline` first doesn't blur the finding, it invents one. Pull the pipeline list before anything else, then run stage distribution, ghost-stage detection and time-in-stage separately per pipeline.

### Stage Distribution
Pull all open deals grouped by stage, one pipeline at a time. Look for:
- Any stage with zero deals in 60 days: candidate for deletion (ghost stage)
- Any stage with average time-in-stage >2x the median sales cycle: deals are being parked here to avoid pipeline review conversations
- Stage definitions: do reps actually know what qualifies a deal to move from Stage X to Stage Y? If entry/exit criteria aren't documented, stage data is subjective

### Closed Lost Reasons
**The fastest fix with the highest long-term ROI — but only once the field is actually structured.**

Check the property type before asking anything about enforcement: `closed_lost_reason` ships on every HubSpot portal as free multi-line text, not a picklist — confirmed both on HubSpot's own admin community and by reading the property schema directly (`get_properties` on deals returns type `string`, no `options`). Nothing constrains reps to a closed set of answers by default, so "is it a required field" is the wrong first question. A required free-text field only guarantees that every closed-lost deal now carries a mandatory sentence in the rep's own words — that is not loss-analysis data, it's a bigger pile of unaggregable prose.

The actual fix is structural: convert the field to a dropdown, or add a separate curated property, with a closed set of reasons that reflects how deals actually die in this business — budget cut after verbal commitment, champion left mid-cycle, lost to a named competitor, no decision ever reached, timing. Require *that* property on Closed Lost, not the free-text one.

**One HubSpot-specific trap when converting the native field in place**: once `closed_lost_reason` switches from text to dropdown, HubSpot won't let the old free-text values populate it on already-closed deals — that history has to be cleared or migrated first, or the field reads as required-and-empty on every deal closed before the conversion. On a portal with real history sitting in the field, adding a new custom picklist property (and leaving the old text field as free-form commentary) is the safer default than converting in place.

**A picklist is not automatically better than the free text it replaces.** A required dropdown with a lazy list just relocates the problem: a rep under deal-review pressure clicks whatever option is fastest, and six months later "strategic intelligence" turns out to be one catch-all value — usually "Price" or "Other" — sitting at 70%+ of every loss. Whichever property ends up holding the answer, read its reason list against how deals actually die in this business before requiring it. A list with one dominant bucket needs redesigning before the field needs enforcing — otherwise the audit trades a visible gap, the blank field, for an invisible one, false precision.

### Forecast Accuracy
Compare last quarter's forecasted pipeline (as of 30 days before quarter end) to actual closed revenue — one pipeline at a time, a blended number across pipelines hides which one is actually broken. Before trusting the variance at all, check how many deals closed in that pipeline that quarter: a handful of deals can swing measured variance further than any of the three causes below ever could, and diagnosing a miscalibrated model off a low-volume quarter is reading a pattern into noise, not finding one. Once the volume behind the number is real, if variance is >20%, either:
- Stage probability settings are wrong — read the actual `hs_deal_stage_probability` on each stage of that pipeline, never the stage label. HubSpot's installed defaults (20/40/60/80/90%) are a starting point almost nobody keeps: stages get renamed, split, and reordered per pipeline, and some portals go as far as baking a percentage into the stage name itself — a stage literally called "Contract sent (50%)" — that has drifted from the real probability field or never matched an actual win-rate calculation in the first place. The name is not the number.
- Close dates are being set optimistically and never updated
- Deals are being entered at the wrong stage

Name which of the three it is before writing the fix plan — the sales lead decides which one to correct first, and only one at a time (see the pipeline-change rule below): fixing probabilities and close-date discipline in the same week makes next quarter's variance impossible to attribute to either.

**There are two forecasts in the portal, and they fail differently.** Everything above audits the probability-weighted forecast, the one HubSpot computes from the stage. Sitting next to it is a second, entirely independent mechanism the reps drive by hand: `hs_manual_forecast_category` — a category each rep sets on the deal themselves (Not forecasted / Pipeline / Best case / Commit / Closed won) — plus `hs_forecast_probability` and `hs_forecast_amount`, a custom percentage and the amount it produces, both distinct from `hs_deal_stage_probability`. Which one the company actually calls "the forecast" in its Monday meeting decides what a variance number even means, so establish that first, then check the two separately:

- **Probability-weighted forecast off, categories sane** → the model is miscalibrated. That is the three causes above.
- **Categories never set, or everything parked in Pipeline** → there is no committed forecast to be wrong. The finding is not accuracy, it's that the field is unused: the number the sales lead defends in the meeting is coming from somewhere outside the CRM, and no CRM fix improves it.
- **Categories set but Commit systematically overshooting** → the model can be perfectly calibrated and the forecast still wrong. This is a rep-judgement and deal-review finding, not a data one, and it belongs in the audit as such rather than being fixed by re-tuning stage probabilities.

Reporting one of these as the other is the most common way a CRM audit sends a team to reconfigure stages when the actual problem was that nobody fills in the forecast category — or the reverse.

**A fourth native field sits next to both forecasts, and it is neither of them.** `hs_deal_score` — HubSpot's own description: "the predictive deal score calculated by HubSpot AI to score the deal health" — is a number field with no documented scale or bounds, distinct from `hs_deal_stage_probability` and from the manual forecast trio above. It doesn't measure the likelihood of closing; it scores the deal's health, an AI signal closer in nature to the predictive contact score than to a forecast field. Don't fold it into a forecast-accuracy calculation and don't average or sum it with `hs_deal_stage_probability` — check whether it's even populated on this portal first, since the feature depends on a Sales Hub tier the audit shouldn't assume. Cite it as a cross-check a sales lead can pull up before a deal-review conversation, never as a fourth input to the variance number above. Don't grade a single value against an assumed 0–100 scale, either — the schema documents no bounds at all, and a real distribution check (sort deals by this field, both directions) on one live portal came back 4 to 68 across 400 scored deals, nowhere near a full 0–100 spread. Pull this portal's own high and low before calling any one score high, medium or low.
