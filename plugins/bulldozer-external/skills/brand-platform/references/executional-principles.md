# Executional Principles

**One-page brief.** Extract a single page from this document — positioning statement, three pillar names with one-line claims, and the on-brand/off-brand word list — and put it at the top of every creative, sales, and content brief. Nobody reads the full platform before writing a landing page or a cold email; they read the one-pager. If the one-pager can't fit on one page, the messaging architecture is still too broad. The full document ships as Markdown; if the one-pager itself needs to circulate as something people open in a meeting or print (not just paste into another brief), produce it as an actual one-page artifact rather than a trimmed Markdown file — the `pdf-report` skill for a print-ready page, or `slides-deck` for a single-slide version teams can drop into a deck.

**Ownership and change control.** Name one owner (usually the person who ran this build). Every other team can propose a change to a pillar, a proof point, or a voice example — nobody else edits the positioning statement or the pillar set directly. A brand platform that anyone can silently rewrite stops being a shared reference within a quarter.

**Where deviation is allowed, and where it isn't.** Tone can and should flex by channel and context (Step 4's tone table exists for this reason). The positioning statement, the three pillars, and the anti-claims do not flex — a rep softening an anti-claim to close a deal, or a content writer inventing a fourth pillar for one blog post, is exactly the drift this document exists to prevent. Flag it, don't let it become a second version of the brand.

Not every drift is the same severity. A rep softening an anti-claim on one live call is a coaching conversation — it happened, it's over, note it and move on. The same softening surviving into a deck template, a standard email sequence, or a case study that then gets reused for months is a document-level problem: the owner needs to correct the asset itself, because every future rep who opens that template inherits the drift as if it were approved.

**Refresh trigger, not a refresh schedule.** Don't put this on a calendar (annual brand refresh is a theater exercise). Revisit the positioning statement when one of these actually happens: the primary buyer changes (moving upmarket, new segment), a proof point central to a pillar stops being true (the category-defining customer churns, the flagship metric changes), or a competitor's repositioning makes the current statement no longer defensible under the Step 2 tests. Revisit voice and tone far less often than positioning — voice drifting slowly as the company matures is normal; positioning drifting is a sign nobody owns it.

**Governance for AI-assisted output.** Reps and marketers increasingly use AI tools to draft outreach, ad copy, and one-pagers at volume. Feed the one-pager (not the full document) into those tools as grounding context, and spot-check a sample of AI-drafted output against the anti-claims and the off-brand word list before it ships — drift compounds faster when it's machine-generated at scale than when one writer goes off-script.
