# Signal Interpretation Rules and Common Mistakes

## Signal Interpretation Rules

**Rule: Distinguish hiring intent from execution.** A single VP of EMEA Sales hire signals intent to enter a market. Ten SDR hires in London signals execution of an already-committed market entry. The strategic implication is different — one gives you 12 months of lead time, the other gives you 3.

**Rule: Headcount freeze before layoffs.** The sequence is predictable: (1) posting velocity drops, (2) non-essential roles disappear, (3) backfill-only hiring, (4) freeze, (5) layoffs 2-4 months later. Catching a competitor in stage 1 or 2 is competitive intelligence that sales teams can use on calls immediately.

**Rule: The executive background check.** Within 48 hours of any Director+ hire at a competitor, check the new hire's last two employers on LinkedIn. This is the same move as the Executive background rule in Signal Type 3 — do it here as a standing habit, not only when a brief is due, because the 48-hour window is when the information is freshest and easiest to attribute to the hire rather than to unrelated news.

**Rule: A reposted listing is not a new signal.** A posting that disappears and reappears within the analysis window is not a fresh hire commitment — it is a company struggling to fill a role, which is the opposite interpretation. Bulldozer OS's Republished Job Offers signal can't catch this for a specific tracked competitor (it's a market-wide discovery filter, not scoped to a company list — see Step 1): the repost has to be caught by hand, by comparing this month's Job Offers Watchlist leads against last month's on title and location for the same competitor. Track a caught repost as its own category: "can't fill X" is a different strategic tell than "committing budget to X," and conflating the two under a single "GTM Expansion" count inflates the signal.

**Rule: A hiring signal without a funding or revenue signal behind it is a hypothesis, not a fact.** Step 4's cross-reference exists because posting volume alone cannot distinguish a well-funded expansion from a company overhiring ahead of revenue. Only promote a signal from "watch" to the Executive Summary once at least one corroborating source — funding, a public revenue disclosure, a customer count claim — points the same direction.

**Rule: Absence of a signal is not evidence of stability.** A layoff doesn't produce a hiring signal at all, and Bulldozer OS has no way to watch a specific tracked competitor for departures either — the Employee Departure signal (`SIGNAL_TYPE_EMPLOYEE_DEPARTURE`) is the same kind of market-wide discovery filter as Company/Department Growth (see Step 1), not something that can be pointed at a named list. So a quiet month proves only that nothing the taxonomy is built to catch happened, on the one channel (Job Offers Watchlist) that is actually watching this competitor at all — it is not proof the competitor is stable, and the brief should not imply it is. When a competitor goes quiet after a period of active hiring, say that the signal has stopped, not that the underlying situation has improved.

**Rule (sales): never repeat a signal to a prospect as a confirmed fact.** A "watch signal" is a hypothesis by definition (see the rule above), and even a confirmed pattern is built from public postings, not from anything the competitor has said directly — a rep who states it on a call as settled fact and turns out to be wrong (a ghost job, a title-inflated role, a signal that reversed before the call happened) spends credibility on the deal, not just on the claim. Two guardrails for the "For Sales" recommendation in the brief format below: state the confidence level the brief itself assigned (confirmed pattern vs. single watch signal), and never frame it as "we've been monitoring their hiring" — to a prospect, that reads as surveillance of the competitor they may still be evaluating, not as market awareness. Lead with the implication for the prospect's own decision, not with the research method behind it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reporting job titles instead of interpretation.** "Competitor X posted 4 SDR roles" is data. "Competitor X is building top-of-funnel capacity before their next fundraise announcement, expect their outbound volume to double next quarter" is intelligence. If a bullet in the brief could be copy-pasted straight from a job board, rewrite it.
- **Treating a small competitor's single hire like a large competitor's ten.** The 3-role threshold is a floor, not a universal unit — a 40-person competitor posting 3 roles in one function is a much bigger relative move than the same 3 roles at a 2,000-person company. State headcount context next to every threshold-based call.
- **Letting the brief go stale between sends.** A signal caught in week 1 and reported in week 4 has already lost most of its lead-time value for sales and product. Read the signal's leads as they arrive rather than batching everything at month end.
- **Skipping Step 4 because the pattern "looks obvious."** The most confident-sounding interpretations (an executive hire "obviously" means X) are exactly the ones cross-referencing most often overturns.
- **Tracking a competitor nobody actually loses deals to.** A market-map entry isn't the same as a live threat — see "Before You Add a Competitor to This Brief." A brief with three padded snapshots reads as more thorough than one with one real threat and an honest note that the rest are quiet; it isn't.
- **Reading two straight quiet months as "they're stable."** The taxonomy catches postings and growth percentages, not layoffs or internal trouble — see the "absence of a signal" rule. Silence is the absence of the one kind of evidence this brief collects, not evidence of the opposite.
