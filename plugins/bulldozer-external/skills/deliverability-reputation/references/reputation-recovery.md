# Reputation Recovery — Phases and Recover-or-Replace Decision

## Step 6 — Reputation Recovery

If reputation is already damaged (Postmaster shows a failing Compliance Status or a bottom-of-scale Deliverability Analysis verdict, inbox placement <60%):

**Phase 1: Stop and diagnose (days 1–3)**
- Pause all sending from the affected domain — every mailbox, every sequencer, every warmup tool. Continuing to send while Compliance Status is failing or the Deliverability Analysis verdict is at the bottom of the scale teaches the filter you're the problem, and resets the recovery clock every time.
- Pull the Google Postmaster Tools graphs for the last 30 days: spam rate, Compliance Status history, Deliverability Analysis verdict trend, and delivery errors. Look for the day the trend turned and cross-reference it against a send: volume spike, a new list segment, a new template, a new mailbox added without warmup.
- Check blacklist status on the sending domain and its IPs (MXToolbox blacklist check or equivalent). A blacklist listing has a separate delisting process per list — recovering domain reputation does not automatically clear a blacklist entry, and vice versa.
- Re-verify authentication is still fully valid (Step 1). A DNS record that silently broke — an expired CNAME, an ESP migration that didn't update SPF — is a common, boring cause of a sudden drop, and it's the fastest one to rule out.

**Phase 2: Fix the root cause (days 3–10)**
- If the cause is list quality: run full re-verification on the entire active list (not just the next campaign's list), remove everything the verifier flags, and remove every contact with any complaint or unsubscribe history that's still active.
- If the cause is content: check the last few templates for spam-trigger patterns (excessive links, urgency language, image-only emails with no text) and check whether reply rate collapsed alongside the spam signal — a sudden drop in replies with a rise in complaints usually means the messaging itself changed, not just the list.
- If the cause is volume: confirm the send history against the volume limits in Step 3. A domain that jumped tiers faster than its warmup schedule allowed is the single most common self-inflicted cause.
- Fix whichever cause the diagnosis points to before sending anything else. Resuming volume before the root cause is fixed just repeats the drop.

**Phase 3: Controlled rebuild (weeks 2–5)**
- Resume sending only to the most-engaged segment: contacts who opened or replied in the last 30 days. Sending to a cold or stale segment first, right after a reputation drop, reproduces the same signal that caused it.
- Restart at the volume of a brand-new domain (Step 3's Week 1–2 numbers), even though this domain has history — reputation has to be rebuilt, not resumed.
- Check Google Postmaster daily during the rebuild. Only move up a volume tier once Compliance Status is passing, the Deliverability Analysis verdict has moved up at least one step, and spam complaint rate has stayed under 0.1% for at least a week at the current tier.
- Recovery is measured in weeks, not days: industry deliverability guidance in 2026 puts a full return to a healthy verdict at roughly 4–8 weeks of clean sending for a moderately damaged domain, and 3–4 weeks of reduced-volume sending before any improvement shows at all for one at the bottom of the scale (`ABUSE_OR_SPAM`). These figures predate the June 2026 Deliverability Analysis rollout and still haven't been re-measured against the new verdict labels specifically — treat as a planning range, not a guarantee. One narrower signal does have a firmer basis, straight from Gmail's own sender guidelines FAQ: mitigation eligibility on the spam-rate check specifically returns once the rate has held below 0.3% for **7 consecutive days**, and the Compliance Status dashboard itself can lag up to 7 days behind a real fix before it reflects the improvement — don't read a still-red dashboard on day 2–3 of clean sending as a sign the fix didn't work. That 7-day figure covers only the spam-rate compliance check, not a full Deliverability Analysis verdict recovery, which remains the unaudited 4–8 week range above. Set both expectations with whoever is waiting on the campaign before starting the rebuild.

**Recover or replace — the decision that matters most:**
If the damaged domain is a dedicated outbound domain (Step 2's architecture, not the primary brand domain), rehab is not always the right call. Standing up a fresh domain and running the standard warmup protocol (Step 3) is commonly faster than a full rehab of a domain sitting at a failing Compliance Status or a bottom-of-scale Deliverability Analysis verdict — call it roughly half the time, sometimes less. If the damaged domain is the primary company domain, replacement isn't an option: recovery is mandatory, because that domain also carries transactional and internal mail. This is exactly why Step 2 says never to send cold outbound from the primary domain — a disposable outbound domain turns a reputation crisis into a same-week decision instead of a two-month recovery project.

**Weigh it as bounded cost vs. open-ended cost, not as a coin flip.** A fresh domain has a known price: registration, mailbox setup, and a warmup cycle whose length you can already read off the table in Step 3. A rehab has no such ceiling — Phase 3 above can stall indefinitely if the root cause wasn't fully fixed, and every day it drags is a day the sequence isn't running. When the two are close, the bounded option wins by default.

**The threshold that should end a rehab attempt, not just slow it down:** if a domain drops back to a failing Compliance Status or a bottom-of-scale Deliverability Analysis verdict a second time within the weeks right after a completed Phase 3 rebuild, stop rehabbing it — don't restart Phase 1. A single drop can be one bad list pull or one broken DNS record; a second drop on the same domain, freshly rebuilt, means the diagnosis in Phase 2 missed the real cause, and a third rehab cycle is very unlikely to find it where the first two didn't. Replace instead, and only revisit the old domain, if ever, once the actual cause has surfaced elsewhere.
