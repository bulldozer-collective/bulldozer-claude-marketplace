# Common Mistakes

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the proposal as a summary of capabilities instead of a summary of the prospect's situation.** A proposal that could be sent to any prospect in the same industry, unchanged, has failed at its one job.
- **Inventing numbers to make the ROI model look more precise.** A conservative estimate built from numbers the prospect gave in discovery is more persuasive than an impressive projection they can't verify — and it survives scrutiny from a stakeholder who wasn't in the room.
- **Sending the proposal cold, by email, with no scheduled walkthrough.** This is the single highest-leverage mistake in the whole process — it turns a document that should be defended live into one that's judged in isolation, usually against a lower-priced alternative.
- **Burying the ask.** A proposal with three possible next steps ("let's talk," "let me know if you have questions," "or just sign whenever you're ready") gives the champion nothing concrete to act on.
- **Skipping the champion/economic-buyer distinction.** A proposal written for the person in the room, when that person cannot approve the spend, leaves the actual decision-maker with an unanswered set of questions to fill in on their own — usually filled in with the cheapest interpretation available. If HubSpot is connected, `hs_buying_role` on the associated contacts answers this directly instead of guessing from who showed up to discovery calls — remember it's multi-select, so don't assume a contact tagged Champion is ruled out from also being the Budget Holder.
- **Re-sending the proposal as the entire follow-up.** It signals nothing has changed and gives the recipient no new reason to reply.
- **Building the full document before checking who can sign it.** A single-threaded deal (one contact on the account, no verified economic buyer) doesn't need a better proposal — it needs the AE to get a second contact before more writing time goes in.
- **Manufacturing a fake deadline to force urgency.** A discount that "expires Friday" for no real reason reads as pressure to an experienced buyer, not information — and it's a well-known tactic, so it costs credibility rather than buying speed.
- **Writing a fourth pricing revision instead of asking what changed.** Repeated price-only revisions with no new information about scope or budget rarely get resolved by a better document.
- **Reaching for a stranger's case study on a renewal or expansion.** When `dealtype` is Existing Business, this account's own results since signing are stronger proof than any comparable client — skipping that history to reuse the New Business format signals the account team hasn't been tracking the relationship.
