# Common Mistakes

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the health score as a report instead of a router.** If a red score doesn't automatically open a playbook and assign an owner within 24 hours, the "system" is a spreadsheet with extra steps.
- **Running QBRs on accounts that don't need them.** A QBR built for an enterprise account and copy-pasted onto every account in the book burns CSM time the score should have already told you not to spend there.
- **Expanding a red or amber account to hit a quarterly number.** It closes the deal and accelerates the churn the health score was built to prevent.
- **Importing a market NRR benchmark as the target.** See Phase 4 above — the number that matters is this book's own churn-vs-score relationship, not a published percentile.
- **Reading a rising NRR as proof the expansion program is working, without checking gross or logo retention next to it.** See Phase 4 — a widening NRR/GRR gap on a seat- or usage-priced book usually means expansion is covering for churn, not fixing it.
- **Letting champion tracking lapse between QBRs.** Champion departure is one of the fastest-moving risk signals in this model; it doesn't wait for the next scheduled review.
- **Scoring a disconnected dimension as if it were measured and red.** See Step 1 — a health score that punishes an account for a data source nobody wired up manufactures false reds and burns CSM trust in the score faster than any single wrong call does.
- **Building the Day-2 recovery plan around the customer's first stated reason.** "Too expensive" or "not using it enough" is a starting point to verify against usage data, not a diagnosis to plan against — see the Red Account Playbook.
- **Pitching expansion to a green account that's still single-threaded.** A health score measures the relationship's condition, not its resilience — a single associated contact can carry a green score right up until they leave.
