# Ad Formats — the 12 shapes a video ad takes

Called from Step 1 of the main framework. Pick the one that drives the structure of the
ad — most real ads combine two formats, but one of them decides how the scenes are
sequenced, and that's the one to classify by. Note the secondary format alongside it
instead of forcing a single label.

## 1. UGC Talking Head

One person speaks directly to a phone-style camera, handheld or on a cheap tripod, for
the whole ad. Structure: hook line straight to camera → problem/context → product
reveal → benefit(s) → CTA, all carried by the same speaker. Identifying features:
vertical framing, visible imperfection (room background, non-studio lighting, phone-mic
audio), first-person language ("I tried this for two weeks"). The format's entire
credibility rests on it *not* looking like an ad — polish works against it.

## 2. Product Demo

Hands-on use of the product showing its actual function, usually without a continuous
on-camera presenter. Structure: the product in use → the specific feature being shown →
the result it produces, repeated per feature. Identifying features: close-up product
shots, voiceover or text overlay doing the explaining instead of a face, cutaways to the
result (the clean surface, the fitted part, the finished dish).

## 3. Before/After

A transformation shown through a split-screen or a hard cut between a "before" state and
an "after" state, with the product as the only variable between them. Structure: before
state (established fast, often under 2s) → the product's intervention → after state held
longer than the before, since it's the payoff. Identifying features: matched camera
angle/framing between the two states so the comparison reads instantly, a visible
timestamp or counter in some variants ("Day 1" / "Day 30").

## 4. Testimonial Compilation

Several customer reviews, cut together, usually with on-screen star ratings or quoted
review text. Structure: a cold open on the strongest line from one testimonial → rotating
through 3–6 more, each shortened to its single best sentence → a closing shot that returns
to the product. Identifying features: inconsistent recording setups across speakers (a
tell that they're real, unscripted reviews, not a single shoot), screenshots of star
ratings or review platforms layered over the footage.

## 5. Problem-Solution

A relatable problem stated or dramatized, immediately followed by the product positioned
as the answer. Structure: problem (agitated just enough to create tension, not dwelt on)
→ pivot line → product as solution → quick proof or demonstration → CTA. Identifying
features: the pivot is usually a single sentence or a hard visual cut — the format fails
if the problem section runs too long relative to the solution.

## 6. Listicle

A numbered list of points — reasons, tips, mistakes, features — delivered in sequence,
each with its own short beat. Structure: cold open naming the list ("3 reasons you're
still doing X wrong") → point 1 → point 2 → point 3(+) → CTA. Identifying features: an
on-screen number or counter per point, a consistent visual template repeated per item
(same text position, same transition), points ordered either by strength (strongest
first, for hook retention) or by narrative build (weakest to strongest, for payoff).

## 7. Unboxing

The product's packaging arriving, being opened, and the first reaction to it. Structure:
package reveal → opening (often slowed or lingered on) → first look at the product →
reaction shot → a quick use/demo tacked on at the end. Identifying features: tactile
close-ups of the packaging itself (not just the product), an audible or visible reaction
beat that reads as unscripted.

## 8. Day-in-the-Life

The product appears naturally inside a daily routine rather than being the subject of
its own scene. Structure: a sequence of routine moments (waking up, commuting, working,
winding down) with the product folded into one or more of them, no dedicated "ad moment."
Identifying features: a vlog-style continuous voiceover or diary captions, the product
treated as incidental rather than announced — the pitch is implicit in showing it fit
naturally into a life the viewer recognizes.

## 9. Comparison

A side-by-side contrast between the product and a competitor, or between the product and
"the old way" of doing something. Structure: the alternative shown first (its friction or
cost) → the product shown doing the same job → an explicit side-by-side or split-screen
moment that makes the contrast visual, not just stated. Identifying features: matched
shot composition between the two sides (same angle, same pacing) so the eye can compare
directly, often a literal split-screen or a wipe transition between them.

## 10. Story Arc

A short narrative with a character, a conflict, and a resolution, structured like a
micro-film rather than a pitch. Structure: setup (character + situation) → conflict/stakes
→ the product's role in resolving it → resolution, with the sell folded into the story
rather than stated separately. Identifying features: a named or implied protagonist,
dialogue or voiceover that reads as narration rather than copy, a resolution beat that
lands emotionally before the CTA appears.

## 11. Text-on-Screen

Animated text carries the message over B-roll, with no presenter speaking to camera (a
voiceover may still be present). Structure: headline text → supporting text per point →
CTA text, each synced to a change in the B-roll underneath it. Identifying features: text
is the primary information channel (the ad works muted), B-roll is illustrative rather
than narrative, typography and animation style do a lot of the tonal work a presenter
would otherwise carry.

## 12. Mashup/Montage

Rapid cuts, each shot held under ~2 seconds, building energy through sheer editing pace
rather than through narrative or explanation. Structure: no fixed beats — the format *is*
the pacing, built from a pool of short clips (product, lifestyle, reactions, text flashes)
cut to music or a beat. Identifying features: cut frequency is the single most diagnostic
signal (count the cuts — if average shot length is under 2s for most of the ad, it's this
format), minimal spoken explanation, often built around a music drop or beat sync.

## Hybrids

Most ads combine two of these — a UGC Talking Head that ends in a Product Demo, a
Problem-Solution that resolves into a Before/After. Classify by whichever format decides
the scene order (Step 3 of the main framework), and note the secondary format in the same
line: "Primary: Problem-Solution. Secondary: Before/After (resolution beat)." Never
invent a 13th category to avoid picking — if nothing fits, pick the closest primary and
describe the deviation in prose instead.
