# Compliance and Accessibility

**Google SEO**: Intrusive interstitials on mobile remain a quality signal Google's ranking systems weigh — avoid a full-screen popup covering content immediately on load. Cookie notices and age/legal verification stay exempt. Note: Search Console's dedicated "Page Experience" report was retired in November 2023 (its data folded into Core Web Vitals and HTTPS reporting) — there's no longer a standalone dashboard for this specific penalty, but the underlying guidance hasn't been withdrawn.

**GDPR / ePrivacy**: Clear consent language, link to the privacy policy, never a pre-checked opt-in. Since 2024, French and European regulators have moved from *guidance* to *enforcement* on one specific point: **"Refuse" must be exactly as easy to find and act on as "Accept"** — same visual weight, same level, no extra click, no color/size trick that nudges toward accepting. The CNIL issued formal notices to about twenty sites in December 2024 over cookie banners where "Refuse" was less visible than "Accept," and on September 3, 2025 fined Google a total of €325M (€200M on Google LLC, €125M on Google Ireland Limited) and Shein €150M — Google's grievance covered both ads displayed inside Gmail without consent and advertising cookies dropped at account creation without valid consent; CNIL's own site still isn't reachable to read the decision directly from this environment, but the €325M total and the two-entity breakdown now converge across multiple independent press sources, not just marketing aggregators. The EDPB's Cookie Banner Taskforce guidance (no pre-ticked boxes, symmetric button treatment) has been enforced in several EU countries since early 2024. Treat an asymmetric Accept/Refuse pair as a compliance defect, not a style choice.

**Button symmetry isn't the whole enforcement target — what fires after the click matters as much.** The same round of CNIL enforcement reportedly treated a mismatch between the "Refuse" click and actual tracker behavior — trackers still firing after a visitor declined — as an aggravating factor, not just the button styling. A popup that carries its own marketing pixel (Meta Pixel, a retargeting tag) needs that pixel gated on real consent state, not fired unconditionally the moment the popup renders; visually symmetric buttons don't help if the decline click doesn't actually stop the tag.

**Dark patterns — a live enforcement risk, not just a brand call**: manufactured urgency ("Only 2 left," a countdown timer that resets), confirmshaming decline copy, and consent flows where "refuse" is visually harder to find than "accept" are now named targets of regulators, not just of UX critique. The FTC settled with Amazon for $2.5B in September 2025 over dark-pattern subscription flows — its largest rule-violation settlement to date — and continues to enforce under FTC Act Section 5. California's CCPA regulations, effective January 1, 2026, explicitly name false urgency as a prohibited dark pattern in consent flows. Apply this to every popup in this skill, not only cookie-consent ones: an exit-intent discount with a fake countdown, or decline copy designed to shame, carries real legal exposure now.

**Accessibility**: Keyboard navigable (Tab, Enter, Esc to close). Focus trap while open. Text contrast at least 4.5:1 against its background (3:1 for large text ≥18pt/24px, and for the close icon itself) — WCAG 2.2 AA, SC 1.4.3 / SC 1.4.11. Measure it, don't eyeball it.

**Focus Not Obscured (WCAG 2.2 AA, SC 2.4.11)** — this one is easy to miss because it's new and it targets exactly the non-modal shapes this skill builds. Whatever holds keyboard focus has to stay at least partially visible on screen. A modal popup that traps focus inside itself (above) already satisfies this — focus can't land under it. A **non-modal** element that doesn't trap focus does not get that shortcut: the announcement banner, a persistent bottom bar, or any popup left rendered while the visitor keeps tabbing through the rest of the page underneath it must never fully cover a focusable element on that page. WCAG's own worked example for this criterion is a sticky banner hiding whatever sits behind it — the exact shape of the Announcement Banner below.

**Redundant Entry (WCAG 2.2 A, SC 3.3.7)** — another additive 2.2 criterion, and it lands directly on a capture popup: don't ask the visitor to re-type something they already gave you earlier in the same session. If a page form already captured the visitor's email (or the visitor is logged in, or a cookie sync already identifies them) and an exit-intent or lead-magnet popup then renders on the same visit, pre-fill the email field with the known value rather than presenting it empty — the visitor can still edit it. This is exactly the same discipline as *"Exclude converted users" should mean known contacts, not just email subscribers* above (don't treat an identified visitor as anonymous); SC 3.3.7 is the accessibility name for not forcing them to redo the typing when they've already been identified.
